# MOO Engine: Recovering the Implicit Values Behind Singapore's COE Policy

## 0. Read this first

This is a personal project with a three week budget, built solo. The deliverable is a
deployed link, a public repo, and a written case study. The audience is product hiring
managers, not academics.

The optimiser is not the point. Non-dominated sorting is a solved problem and pymoo does
it in one call. What is being demonstrated here is the ability to take a contested public
argument, force it into an explicit formulation, and be honest about what that
formulation cost. Every shortcut taken in the model must be visible in the output, not
buried in a notebook.

**The single headline claim this project exists to support:** Singapore's COE settings
imply a specific ratio of how much the state values congestion relief against car
affordability against revenue. That ratio was never published. It can be recovered from
public data.

---

## 1. The argument

People reject viable policies because they optimise for different values, then mistake a
difference in preference for an objective failure. There is a real category of failed
policy, and there is a much larger category of policy that is simply weighted
differently, and public argument does not distinguish between them.

Pareto efficiency draws the line. A policy that is worse on every objective than some
other available policy is a genuine failure, and no value system rescues it. A policy on
the frontier is one where you cannot improve any objective without giving up another, and
whether it is the right answer depends entirely on what you weight.

COE is the test case because the Singapore public argument about it is exactly this
confusion. A tradesman who needs a van, a family that wants a car, and a commuter who
wants clear roads all read the others' preferred settings as proof the system is broken.
They are describing three different points on the same frontier.

## 2. Scope

### In scope

- One scenario: the Certificate of Entitlement and Vehicle Quota System, Categories A, B
  and C
- Three objectives
- Continuous optimisation over a small decision space, front generated by NSGA-II
- The inverse weight query and its ternary rendering
- A static deployed frontend
- A written case study

### Explicitly out of scope

- User-uploaded datasets. There is one scenario and it is curated.
- A database. Nobody logs in and nothing persists. Do not add Supabase or any other
  store.
- A live backend. Everything is computed at build time.
- Categories D and E. Motorcycles and the open category add complexity without adding to
  the argument.
- Any claim to be forecasting COE prices. This is not that and must not be presented as
  that.

## 3. The model

### 3.1 Decision variables

Three continuous variables, one per category, each an annual quota count:

- `q_a` Category A quota
- `q_b` Category B quota
- `q_c` Category C quota

Bounds should be set from the historical range in the LTA data, extended somewhat at both
ends so the frontier is not clipped by the bounds themselves. Record the chosen bounds and
the reason in the case study.

Using three separate quotas rather than a single total is not cosmetic. If quota were one
number, every objective would be a function of that one number and the "frontier" would
collapse into a single traced line rather than a surface. The category split is what makes
this genuinely multi-objective, because the categories have different demand behaviour and
different contributions to road load.

### 3.2 Objectives

All three are computed from the decision variables through the fitted models in section 4.
Express everything in minimisation form internally, and flip signs at the presentation
layer.

**O1. Ownership cost.** Minimise the volume-weighted mean quota premium across the three
categories. This is the buyer's objective.

**O2. Congestion.** Minimise. Derived by propagating the quota into a car population, the
population into road load, and the load into an average speed or delay figure. See 4.3.

**O3. Revenue.** Maximise total premium collected, which is the sum over categories of
quota times premium.

The interesting structure comes from O3 being non-monotone. Cost is minimised by flooding
the market with quota. Congestion is minimised by choking it. Revenue is quota times
price, and since price falls as quota rises, revenue peaks somewhere in the middle. The
frontier has a shape rather than a slope.

### 3.3 Constraints

Constraints are where value judgements openly live, and they must be kept separate from
dominance in both the code and the UI. A policy excluded by a constraint is not a failed
policy. It is a policy you ruled out.

At minimum:

- A floor on Category C quota, so goods vehicles are not optimised to zero. Pick a
  defensible floor and say where it came from.
- Bounds on each quota as above.

Every constraint carries a one line justification in the config, and the frontend shows
constraint-excluded and dominated regions in visually distinct ways.

## 4. What has to be fitted, and how honest to be about it

This is the section that decides whether the project is credible. Nothing here may be
invented. Every functional form is either fitted to published data or cited to
literature, and where a form is chosen for tractability rather than accuracy, that is
stated in the output rather than in a footnote.

### 4.1 Quota to premium

The relationship between quota released and clearing premium is fitted from LTA bidding
results. The series runs from roughly 2002 under the open bidding system, twice monthly,
per category, giving several hundred paired observations.

Start with a reduced-form log-log specification per category:

```
ln(P_i) = a_i + b_i * ln(Q_i) + controls
```

Consider including a lagged premium term and a time trend. Try a few specifications and
report the comparison rather than presenting the winner alone.

**The caveat that must ship with this.** COE premiums are a bid outcome, not a function of
quota. They move on demand shifts, deregistration cycles, category migration, credit
conditions and policy announcements. Quota is also plausibly endogenous, since LTA adjusts
it in response to market conditions. A fitted curve captures central tendency and nothing
else, and this is a reduced form, not a structural demand estimate.

Say all of that plainly in the case study and in a tooltip on the chart. Stating the
limitation is a stronger move than pretending the model is tight, and it feeds directly
into the sensitivity work in section 6.

Also note the structural breaks the data contains: the closed to open bidding transition
in 2002, the suspension of bidding exercises in April, May and June 2020, and the May 2022
recategorisation of Category A and B around electric vehicle power output. Decide how to
handle each and document the decision.

### 4.2 Quota to vehicle population

A COE runs ten years, so the vehicle stock is roughly a rolling window of the last decade
of registrations. Model the stock as a simple accumulator: current population, minus
deregistrations, plus the quota being released. Validate the accumulator by running it
forward over the historical record and comparing against the published population series.
If it does not reproduce history, the accumulator is wrong and the frontier built on top
of it is worthless.

### 4.3 Population to congestion

Use the standard BPR volume-delay function rather than inventing a relationship:

```
speed = free_flow_speed / (1 + alpha * (V/C)^beta)
```

with the conventional alpha and beta, cited. Calibrate capacity from published road length
and whatever observed speed figures LTA publishes. Where a parameter cannot be calibrated
from data, set it explicitly in a config file with a comment saying it is an assumption,
and include it in the sensitivity sweep.

## 5. The engine

### 5.1 Front generation

pymoo, NSGA-II, three objectives, three decision variables. This is a small problem and
should run in seconds.

Alongside the frontier, sample the feasible space densely and keep the full cloud. The
cloud is not debug output, it is the product. Showing every possible policy at once, with
the frontier as the boundary of what is achievable, is a far better rendering of the
argument than a filtered table would be. The dominated interior is what a "0" looks like.

### 5.2 The inverse weight query

For each point on the front, determine the set of weight vectors under which that point is
the optimal choice.

Implementation: sample the weight simplex on a grid. For each weight vector, compute the
weighted sum across normalised objectives and record which front point wins. Store the
mapping. This is a nested loop over two precomputed arrays and runs offline. No solver
needed.

With three objectives the weight vector has three components summing to one, which is a
triangle, so the entire preference space renders as a ternary plot coloured by winning
policy. Plotly supports ternary charts directly.

Two things to handle rather than hide:

- **Normalisation matters here.** Dominance is invariant to monotone rescaling of any
  single objective, but weighted sums are not. The ternary map depends on how the three
  objectives were normalised onto a common scale. Pick a method, min-max is fine, and
  state it prominently. This is a place where the tool's own value judgement enters and it
  should be labelled as such.
- **Concavity.** Points in a concave region of the front win under no weight vector at
  all, because a weighted sum cannot reach into a concavity. Those policies are
  non-dominated yet never optimal under any linear preference. That is a real result and
  it is philosophically interesting. Detect these points, mark them differently in the UI,
  and write about them.

### 5.3 The headline output

Locate Singapore's actual current quota settings in the decision space, find the nearest
point on the generated frontier, and read off the implied weight region. Report the
recovered ratio.

Do this carefully. If the actual policy sits well off the frontier under this model, that
is a finding about the model's omissions rather than a finding about LTA, and it must be
reported that way. Do not spin a model failure into a policy critique.

### 5.4 Validation

Before any of the above is trusted, the optimiser is validated against a benchmark with an
analytically known front. Use ZDT1 for the two-objective sanity check and DTLZ2 for the
three-objective case. Compare generated points against the known front and report the
error.

This is a test, not a scenario. It needs no UI and lives in the test suite. It exists so
that the answer to "how do you know the optimiser is right" is not "the picture looked
plausible."

## 6. Sensitivity

This is a required feature, not an extra. Everything downstream rests on fitted
coefficients that carry real uncertainty.

Perturb the fitted coefficients across their confidence intervals, regenerate the frontier
some number of times, and render the result as a band rather than a line. Where the band
is narrow, the conclusion is robust. Where it is wide, say so.

Then do the same for the headline claim. If the recovered weight ratio moves from 3:1 to
1.5:1 across plausible parameter values, the honest statement is a range, not a number.

Doing this well is worth more in an interview than a tighter frontier would be.

## 7. Architecture

Static. There is no server.

```
Python build script  ->  JSON artifacts  ->  static frontend
```

The frontier, the feasible cloud, the weight simplex map and the sensitivity bands are all
computed offline and written to JSON. The deployed site loads them and renders. Weight
sliders need no backend because weights only select a point on a front that already
exists.

The trade-off accepted here is that users cannot move constraints and watch the feasible
region reshape. That would require live re-optimisation. It is a real loss and it is worth
it for having no infrastructure to run, pay for or explain.

### Repo layout

```
/data/raw            downloaded LTA files, committed for reproducibility
/data/processed      cleaned series
/src/fit             the fitted models from section 4
/src/model           objectives, constraints, the problem definition
/src/optimise        pymoo runs, front generation, weight simplex map
/src/export          JSON artifact writers
/tests               ZDT1 and DTLZ2 validation, accumulator backtest
/web                 static frontend
/docs                case study, decision log
```

### Stack

- Python with pymoo, numpy, pandas, statsmodels or scikit-learn for the fits
- Plotly for charting, chosen over D3 because the timeline is three weeks
- Static hosting, whatever is quickest to deploy from the repo

Do not add dependencies beyond this without a reason recorded in the decision log.

## 9. Frontend

Three views, in priority order. If time runs out, ship fewer views well rather than all
three badly.

**View 1, the space.** The full feasible cloud in three-objective space with the frontier
on its boundary. Dominated interior styled distinctly from constraint-excluded regions.
This is the view that carries the argument, so it ships first.

**View 2, the ternary map.** The weight simplex coloured by winning policy, with
Singapore's recovered position marked. Clicking a region shows the policy it selects and
the weights it implies. This is the headline, so it ships second.

**View 3, the point inspector.** Click any point on the frontier and see its quota
settings, its three objective values, and what is being given up relative to neighbouring
points.

Weight sliders sit alongside view 1 and move a marker along the frontier. Every chart that
depends on a fitted model carries a visible note saying so.

Design should be plain and legible. This is an analytical tool and the charts are the
content.

## 11. Framing, and what not to claim

Rittel and Webber define wicked problems partly by having no definitive formulation and no
stopping rule. Multi-objective optimisation requires a fixed formulation, fixed objectives
and stable evaluation functions. So this project does not solve a wicked problem. It
forces one into a tame formulation and then makes the choices involved in that forcing
visible and contestable.

That is the more interesting claim anyway, and it is the one to make. Writing "we model
wicked problems" is the first thing anyone who knows the literature will attack.

Three things that must not be claimed:

- That this predicts COE prices
- That the recovered weights are LTA's actual internal reasoning, rather than what the
  policy implies under this specific model
- That a policy outside the frontier under this model is therefore a bad policy

## 12. The case study

Structure it around the reasoning, not the output. Most portfolio projects show what was
built and none show why. The document should cover:

- The argument, stated in a paragraph
- Why COE, and what was rejected. The public health and housing versions were both
  considered and dropped, one because it would have reproduced a published frontier, the
  other because affordability and density mostly do not conflict so there was no frontier
  to plot.
- The scope cuts, with reasons. Three objectives rather than more, because dominance gets
  sparse in higher dimensions and nearly everything becomes non-dominated. Static rather
  than live. One scenario rather than two.
- What was fitted, what was assumed, and what the sensitivity analysis did to the
  conclusions
- The headline result, with its range
- What is wrong with it and what a second version would fix

The decision log is an appendix, not a substitute for this.
